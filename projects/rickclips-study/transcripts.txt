== 1. Slow Eddie (56.1 s)
The fastest gun in the West had one rule: never draw first. Imagine you're standing in the street at noon, staring down a guy everyone calls Slow Eddie. His hands are at his sides. He hasn't even looked at his gun. And he just nods at you and says, "After you." So you grab for your gun faster than you've ever moved in your life, yet you're face down in the dirt before it clears the holster. How is that possible? Eddie isn't even fast. He'd lose a race to the saloon against a guy carrying two beers. But men ride in from three states away to duel him, and every one of them goes down the exact same way. The only weird thing anyone ever notices is that Eddie never looks at your face. He just stares at your hand. You see, think about the last time your phone slid off a table. You caught it before you even realized it was falling, because you never decided to catch it. Your body just did. But when you went for your gun, you had to decide the exact moment to draw. And that tiny decision slowed your hand down. Eddie never decides anything. He just waits for your hand to twitch and lets his body react on its own.

== 2. The memory shop (77.8 s)
He sold his happiest memory to a stranger behind a counter. You see, memories are money here. There's a shop on every corner that'll pull one right out of your skull, landlords take rent in birthdays, and a bag of groceries costs the day you learned to ride a bike. The city is split in two by a giant wall. On one side, the rich just buy everyone else's summers. So some guy over there has been to the Bahamas 300 times without leaving his couch. On the other side, people stare at old photos of themselves, smiling, with no idea why. And since the rich only want the good stuff, the happier the memory, the more it pays. Jerry only sold his to cover rent. But that same week, his little brother gets sick, and every night in the hospital, he begs for the same bedtime story: the summer they caught a frog the size of a football at the lake. But the medicine costs way more than rent. So Jerry goes back and sells that summer for a week of it. The best birthday he ever had pays for the surgery, and soon he's selling whole years, until his head is mostly bad days and bus rides. His brother keeps getting worse, until there's only one good year left, and the guy behind the counter stares at him and says, "Kid, if you sell this, there's nothing happy left in there." He sells it anyway, and it pays for everything. A week later, his brother sits up in bed, cured, and says, "Hey, Jerry, tell me the frog story." And Jerry has no idea who this kid is. He sold every happy year he ever had, and his brother was in every single one.

== 3. The scratch-card guy (63.3 s)
Look at these two scratchcards. One's a winner and one's garbage. Yet there's a way to tell without scratching either of them. It all started when a guy got fired and spent five bucks on two scratch cards on the way home. He scratches one and wins his five bucks back. But instead of celebrating like a normal person, he gets suspicious. If the lottery decides exactly how many winners get printed, how does the machine know which ones they are? So he does what any sane person would do. He blows his entire severance check on a huge stack, spreads them across his apartment floor and stares until something jumps out. Every card has little grids of numbers printed right on the front, and most of the numbers show up two or three times, but a few only show up once. And whenever three of those loners line up in a row, it's a winner. Which means this guy just cracked the lottery. He could walk into every gas station in the state and clean them out. So what does he do? He calls the lottery. "Sir, that's impossible." "Okay." So he walks into their office with a box full of cards he's never scratched, sorted into winners and losers, and gets 90% of them right. By the end of the week, the game is gone from every store.

== 4. The bell on the grave (59.5 s)
They buried a man alive with a bell tied to his finger. You see, people were so scared of waking up in a coffin that graves came with a little bell on top, tied by string to the body's finger. Except his bell starts ringing at two in the morning. The guy next door decides it's probably the wind. Then it rings faster, and now the whole street is out on their porches going, "You go check." "No, you go check." Somebody drags the doctor out of bed, and he goes, "Bodies twitch. He's dead. I signed the paper myself." But his wife isn't on her porch. She's sitting in the dark with a shovel by the door, because she knows that ring. Three short, one long. The exact signal they practiced. Wait. Practiced? Well, he was never dead. He owed money to half the town, so he faked his death. The second that bell rang, she was supposed to grab the shovel, dig him up, and skip town with him. Instead, she just sits there all night, listening to it ring fast, then slow, then once every few minutes, until sunrise, when it finally stops. You see, with him in the ground, every debt got buried right along with him. And the house was all hers.

== 5. The lottery clerk (57.7 s)
He scanned a customer's lottery ticket, saw it had won a million dollars, and said, "Sorry, man. Not a winner." The guy just shrugs, says, "Story of my life," and walks out. The second the door closes, the cashier is elbow deep in the trash can, fishing the ticket back out and tucking it into his wallet like it's been his all along. By midnight, he's already bought a boat, a mansion, and a whole new set of friends, at least in his head. So the next morning, he drives 2 hours to the lottery office, slides the ticket under the glass, and the lady at the counter smiles and says, "Wow, congratulations. Just wait right here." So he waits. 5 minutes go by. Then ten. Until finally, a door opens behind him. In walks the guy from the store. Except now he's wearing a suit with a badge on his belt, followed by two police officers. You see, in real life, lotteries noticed that store clerks were winning way more often than everyone else, which is statistically a little suspicious. So they started printing winning tickets as bait and sending undercover agents into stores pretending to be regular customers.
